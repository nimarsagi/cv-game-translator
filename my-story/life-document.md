## My story

### Chapter 1: The Ordinary World
I began as an enthusiastic, optimistic, curious kid, the one who drew attention in a room and made friends easily. I also made a point of getting to know the classmates others left out or called "weird". Understanding them made me slower to judge anyone. I started secondary school in VMBO, the pre-vocational track. I carried an early calling: to become a doctor, like generations of my family before me. That goal lifted me into HAVO. Somewhere along the way I realised the dream wasn't mine. I was chasing it to make my parents proud, hoping it would make me happy, and it didn't sit right. I kept what was truly mine, the drive to help people, and let go of the inherited role. I finished VWO, the pre-university track, simply because I loved learning. One of my scripts was staged as a theatre play at my school, an early sign of what came next.

### Chapter 2: The Artist's Detour
After VWO I followed a second call: storytelling. I gave myself a year to test it for real. I wrote screenplays, worked on a film set as an assistant producer, and directed my own film, which remains unreleased. That year taught me something harder than any film school could. I loved stories, but not the daily grind of writing, and not the slow climb through low-budget productions. I had wanted to start at the top without paying the entry price. Once I saw that price clearly, I chose not to pay it. Meanwhile, a quieter pursuit took root again: trading.

### Chapter 3: Finding the Real Call
Looking back, I saw what had actually held me in film. It wasn't the writing or the sets; it was the people inside the stories: how minds work, how people struggle, what drives them. I spent hours watching interviews with people facing mental challenges, trying to understand them from the inside. Storytelling had been the vehicle; the human psyche was the destination. In 2023 I enrolled in psychology at the University of Amsterdam.

### Chapter 4: The Mentors and the Gift
At university I found my mentors among the methods professors, the ones who questioned the rules of psychological science instead of just teaching them. From them I took my most important tool: the habit of digging beneath any conclusion to the assumptions and methods holding it up. I tested that tool in the markets. Statistics and machine learning first drew me toward quantitative trading, where I gained experience modelling data. But what I enjoyed most was building a narrative for each trade: "What has to happen for this to work?" Then I attacked my own story: "What would make me take the loss, or cut the profit short?" The storyteller builds the narrative; the methodologist tries to break it.

### Chapter 5: The Call to Adventure
In my first year of psychology, I met the thing that would pull every earlier thread together: AI. Fascination became daily use, and daily use became craft. I wrote prompts for specific kinds of academic work and shared them with my cohort, along with AI summaries that my classmates came to rely on, additionally, I suggested and created a second brain for one of my professors, a theorist, as a response to the theory crisis. I was helping people by building tools that made their work easier. I completed Anthropic's Claude 101 and AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations. 

### Chapter 6: Crossing the Threshold
After graduating in 2026, I faced the expected next step, a master's, and postponed it. I sized the decision like a trading position. Building AI workflows for companies was a field too new for formal curricula, where early movers could gain far more than they risked. So I chose to learn by building. Searching for a method, I found Jake Van Clief's ICM (Interpretable Context Methodology) and joined its community, and later its VIP tier. ICM matched a conviction I took from psychological methods: Occam's razor, the simplest structure that does the job. Where others saw a chatbot, I began seeing workflows.

### Chapter 7: Allies
A conversation about AI after a yoga class led me to my co-founder. His background in consulting, strategy and software engineering covered what I lacked. He saw in me an unusual way of thinking, and someone who acts on ideas instead of just discussing them. Together we began building Milias, an AI workflow consultancy, still in formation. I design the workflows and the division of work between people and AI. Our first engagement was a pro bono pilot for a fruit and vegetable export company.

### Chapter 8: The Ordeal
At twenty, I walked away from film when I saw the struggle it demanded. At twenty-four, I face the same kind of test and make the opposite choice. My ordeal is a daily one: postponing reward while peers take the paid and predictable path. I wake up knowing I have to walk toward what makes me uncomfortable. My ordeal takes two concrete forms. The first was putting myself in public, with a stint of posting content on TikTok and Instagram. The second is earning the right to advise companies at all. I answer it the way I answer every hypothesis: with evidence, building credibility through pro bono pilots until the results speak for me.

### Chapter 9: The Reward
The pilot was pro bono, besides that, I also helped a friend of mine create funnels for e-commerce. These experiences gave me rules I could not have learned any other way:
- Working with AI in Europe means answering to both AI law and sector-specific regulation.
- Security is my responsibility.
- The most elegant AI workflow can still be the wrong one: use traditional code first, and AI only where nothing simpler will do.
- An automation that costs nearly as much as the process it replaces isn't worth building. Optimizing for saving cost is a must. 

Even stepping back from social media was a lesson: first prove myself to myself and to others, then tell the world.

### Chapter 10: Return with the Elixir
My elixir is not AI itself; it is judgment about AI. Where the market sells hype, I offer realism about what is achievable for a particular company and what isn't. I help small and mid-sized businesses avoid investments driven by fear of missing out. I put AI where it genuinely saves costs or raises profits, so they can grow output without growing headcount proportionally. I believe these businesses create much of the world's real value, and helping them adapt is how I contribute in the long run. The boy who once chased a doctor's title found his own way to help people.
